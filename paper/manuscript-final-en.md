# Introduction

### Why this matters


Automated assessment of text origin is applied where the decision carries a cost:
checking student work, moderating platforms, screening contractors, editorial
policies. A verdict of "machine-written" carries consequences for the author, and
so the question of what exactly a detector measures stops being a technical one.

The cost of error is asymmetric. A missed machine text is cheap; a false
accusation against a human is expensive, and the author cannot defend against it:
proving one's own authorship is harder than disputing it. In the accusatory scenario,
therefore, what decides is not average accuracy but the detector's behaviour at a
low false positive rate — and on this characteristic systems diverge dramatically.

### Machine-likeness is not directly observable


The origin of a text is a property of the process, not of the text itself. We observe
only the result, and any machine-likeness score is an operationalisation: a set of
features, a way of weighting them, a decision threshold.

An uncomfortable consequence follows: different operationalisations may diverge,
and this is not necessarily an error on the part of either. Until they are shown to
measure the same thing, the question "which detector is right" is posed before it
can be answered.

This property of the process depends not only on who wrote the text but also on the
conditions under which it was written. A rigid technical specification narrows the
choice of wording; editorial regimentation standardises the structure; a platform
template dictates the formatting. All three push the text in the same direction in
which the machine is presumed to act.

### How authorship is confounded in practice


A corpus for detector evaluation is almost always assembled so that the origin class
coincides with something else.

Human texts are taken from platforms, machine texts are generated through channels —
and the source becomes a proxy for the class. Human texts come from genres where one
regimentation regime applies, machine texts are written to tasks of a different kind —
and the genre becomes a proxy for the class. Human texts keep the publication's
formatting, machine texts arrive from an API without it — and the formatting becomes
a proxy for the class. Finally, both classes pass through a data preparation
pipeline, and its decisions — what counts as a heading, what counts as
prose — change the model input without changing a single word.

Each of these confounds is known in isolation. What happens to a machine-likeness
score when production conditions change while origin stays the same is a separate
question, and it is rarely asked.

### What the literature review established


A systematic sample of 73 papers shows that studies predominantly evaluate detector
accuracy and robustness to editing, while the human side of the comparison
is assembled opportunistically, without control over the task, the regimentation, or
the production channel.

In this sample, no studies were found that separately measure the effect of
production conditions on machine-likeness scores with origin held fixed. Nor were
any estimates found of the false positive rate for formal and translated
Russian-language prose at the same time. Both claims are narrowed to the scope of
the search performed.

### Design


The study compares production conditions under fixed tasks.

The machine part of the corpus was generated by four generation channels — each
backed by its own model family — on 45 tasks in three modes: from a neutral
technical specification to a task requiring the avoidance of typical
machine-text features, with two replicates. The main contrast is computed within the machine corpus: both
halves of a pair are generated by the same model for the same task, and only the
formulation differs. Origin does not change; the conditions do.

The human part consists of archival texts published before 2022, annotated by
source regimentation level before the texts themselves were read. It includes the
stratum on which the cost of error is measured: formal academic prose and translations.

Machine-likeness is measured by four procedures with different inputs and different
provenance of weights: an interpretable index with weights fixed before data
collection; a trained classifier on the same feature matrix with group splits; a
zero-shot metric on an open Russian-language model; a judge model with a rubric.
None is declared the gold standard.

The design, hypotheses, and analysis rules were fixed by preregistration before the
dependent variable was computed; deviations are published as amendments with dates
and reasons.

### Research questions


1. Do text production conditions change its machine-likeness score under a fixed
   task?
2. Do the direction and magnitude of the effect agree across the four
   operationalisations?
3. How strongly do the results depend on the source, genre, register, formatting,
   and transformations of the text?

### Contributions


The study delivers four things.

**A measurement of the effect of conditions with origin held fixed** — on a
Russian-language corpus, with a fixed task and a preregistered contrast.

**A test of the agreement of the four operationalisations** instead of an
assumption of it, with an explicit rule: matching signs count as convergence of
operationalisations, not as independent replication.

**An assessment of how the result depends on conditions that usually stay in the
background** — the source, genre, formal register, formatting, and ten
transformations of the text, including cases where the decision flips while the
text remains unchanged.

**Recorded limitations of transferability** — which of the reported numbers do not
transfer to applications where both classes come from a single source, and why.

The study does not propose a new detector and does not improve existing ones. Its
subject is what the procedures already in use actually measure when text production
conditions change while origin remains the same.

# Research gap

### 1. What the literature measures


Research predominantly evaluates detector accuracy and robustness to editing.

Two findings in this area are firmly established. The first: a universal binary
detector transfers poorly to new models, domains, and attacks — RAID assembled six
million generations across eleven models and eight domains and showed performance
collapsing under a change of decoding strategy and under adversarial attacks (Dugan et al., 2024); on
unfamiliar domains and generators, TPR at 1% FPR falls as low as zero (Tufts et al., 2025); M4
(Wang et al., 2024a) and MULTITuDE across eleven languages (Macko et al., 2023) give the same result. The second:
average accuracy is unfit for the accusatory scenario — in an audit of four
detectors on 1992 excerpts, open systems mistook up to 78% of human text for
machine text, and at an FPR ceiling of 0.5% only one commercial system retained
detection (Jabarian and Imas, 2025).

Borile and Abrate, 2025 name the cause of poor transfer: linguistic and domain confounders
create spurious correlations that do not carry beyond the training distribution.
The question of which specific conditions give rise to these correlations is not posed there.

### 2. How the human side of the comparison is assembled


The human reference is often collected opportunistically — from whatever is available —
without control over the task, the regimentation, or the production channel.

This is not a complaint about individual studies but a consequence of how the field
is set up: to measure a detector, any corpus of demonstrably human texts will do, and
ready-made collections are the most convenient choice. Under such selection, the
conditions in which these texts were written are not recorded and do not enter the analysis.

The cost is visible in Fredrick and Craven, 2025: the direction of differences in lexical diversity changes
with the composition of the human control group. If a machine-likeness score
depends on the conditions under which the reference was written, then a threshold
calibrated on one corpus carries over to another with no guarantees.

### 3. What the sample lacks


**Within the included sample of 73 studies, no research was found that separately
assessed how text production conditions — task mode, regimentation level,
and generation channel — change a text's machine-likeness scores
while its origin remains unchanged.**

Three groups of studies come close, and each stops short. Studies of
editing prevalence count how much text has passed through a model (Liang et al., 2024,
Kobak et al., 2025) but do not ask how much machine-likeness the task itself creates. Work on
hybrid authorship varies the mode of editing (Artemova et al., 2025, Su et al., 2025, Zeng et al., 2024, Baumler et al., 2026) — yet
the human reference in these studies is written freely, without regimentation. Fairness studies
show that the score varies across subgroups of writers (Liang et al., 2023, Stowe et al., 2026, Basu et al., 2026), not across the
conditions under which the text was produced.

CoAT comes closest — a Russian-language corpus of 246 thousand texts and
thirteen generators (Shamardina et al., 2024). It shows directly that the mode of production changes
detectability: machine-translated texts are recognised with an accuracy of 0.621, while
free generation is recognised with 0.99. The study stops
at the same point as the others: the generator changes together with the task type,
so the effect of conditions is not separated from the effect of origin, and the human
side is drawn from the same domains without control over task or regimentation.

Close by stands the study by Gritsay and colleagues (Gritsay et al., 2023): detecting machine-generated fragments in
Russian-language scientific documents yields an F1 of 0.968 with multi-domain training and
paraphrase augmentation, against 0.825 in English. The authors conclude outright
that a universal detector for all domains and languages is unlikely to be achievable — a conclusion
that matches ours in substance and was obtained on different data. There, too, production
conditions are not varied with origin held unchanged.

To this is added a language gap. The matrix contains five Russian-language primary studies,
and none varies production conditions with origin unchanged: RuATD
collects generators (Shamardina et al., 2022), LLMTrace annotates mixed authorship character by character
(Tolstykh et al., 2025), AINL-Eval measures transfer to unseen scientific fields (Batura et al., 2025), CoAT
compares generative task types together with a change of generator (Shamardina et al., 2024), and the study
by Gritsay and colleagues measures transfer between domains of scientific documents (Gritsay et al., 2023).

**Among the included studies, no FPR estimates were found that cover
formal and translated Russian-language prose at the same time.**

Both claims are worded to stay within the scope of the search performed: they speak
of what is absent from the included sample, not of what is absent from the literature at large.

**Where the sample is thinner, this is stated outright.** A targeted follow-up search on 1 August 2026 found two
peer-reviewed Russian-language studies missed by the original search: both were published outside
arXiv and outside international conferences. The original protocol searched mostly by
English keywords in English-language databases, so sample completeness for
Russian-language journal publications is lower than for preprints and conference proceedings.
The follow-up protocol and the analysis of the missed studies are in `01-literature/targeted-journal-sweep-2026-08-01.md`.

### 4. What the present study does


This study closes the gap by comparing production conditions under fixed
tasks and with several non-coinciding operationalisations of machine-likeness.

The task is held fixed while its mode varies — from a rigid technical specification
to a free formulation. Origin does not change: both halves of the
compared pair are produced by the same model from the same task. The human part
is annotated by source regimentation level, and the annotation is done from the
source before the texts are read.

Machine-likeness is measured by four procedures with different inputs and different ways of
obtaining weights: an interpretable index with weights set before data collection;
a trained classifier on the same feature matrix; a zero-shot metric on an
open Russian-language model; and a judge model scoring against a rubric. None is treated as a
gold standard, and the agreement of their signs is tested rather than assumed.

What remains background in the studies listed above is measured separately: FPR on the
formal register and translated prose, stability of the decision under text transformations,
and the contribution of source and genre compared with the contribution of style.

A limitation of the method is named upfront: word lists have a shelf life, because
authors adapt to published markers (Geng and Trotta, 2025). This is an argument for
features not reducible to a word list, and it was taken into account in feature selection.

`protocol-group-crosswalk.md`.*

# Related work

### 1. The detection task and the limits of its solution


A universal binary detector does not transfer beyond the conditions in which it
was tested. RAID assembled six million generations across eleven models and eight
domains and showed that detectors break under a change of decoding strategy and
under adversarial edits (Dugan et al., 2024). A direct test of seven systems yields TPR at FPR 1%
down to zero on unfamiliar domains and generators (Tufts et al., 2025). The same result is
reproduced by M4 (Wang et al., 2024a), MultiSocial on 22 languages (Macko et al., 2025) and MULTITuDE on
eleven (Macko et al., 2023). Borile and Abrate, 2025 name the cause: the detector picks up linguistic
and domain confounders, and these correlations do not transfer. Attempts to make
the decision explainable follow the same path — combining probability curvature
with stylometry and readability metrics, decomposing feature contributions via
SHAP (Marchenko Breneur et al., 2026).

Estimates of how far the task is solved at all diverge. Studies reporting
99.0 F1 cross-domain (Verma et al., 2024) and AUROC 94.92% in OOD scenarios (Chen et al., 2025) measure
quality on the aggregate; studies that break the estimate down by condition see
drops to zero (Tufts et al., 2025) and to 35% under attacks (Wang et al., 2024b). Both groups are right
within their own design, and this is the first thing that gets in the way of
comparing results across papers.

A recurring design limitation: the human side of the comparison is almost always
collected opportunistically. The corpus is taken from ready sources — news,
essays, Wikipedia — and the machine part is generated to match it. The conditions
under which the human text was written are neither fixed nor varied.

Hence the first step towards our question: if detector quality changes with
domain and generator, the next question is whether it changes with the
conditions under which the human was writing.

### 2. Interpretable features and neural detectors


The line-up of methods is set by three classes. Zero-shot detectors on likelihood
statistics: DetectGPT computes probability curvature and raises AUROC from 0.81 to
0.95 on GPT-NeoX news (Mitchell et al., 2023), Fast-DetectGPT replaces perturbation with sampling
and speeds up the computation 340-fold with a relative gain of about 75% (Bao et al., 2024), Binoculars
catches over 90% of ChatGPT generations at FPR 0.01% without training (Hans et al., 2024), GPT-who
measures the uniformity of information density (Venkatraman et al., 2024), DivEye — diversity (Basani et al., 2025).
Trained classifiers: Ghostbuster (Verma et al., 2024), RepreGuard on hidden
representations (Chen et al., 2025), feature-augmented transformers (Mady et al., 2026). The third class is
interpretable features, and it is the main one for us.

The feature-based approach works and requires no access to the model. Stylometry
over lexis, grammar, syntax and punctuation yields MCC .87 in a seven-class task
on ten-sentence descriptions of terms (Przystalski et al., 2026). On
American Chemical Society papers, a classifier on off-the-shelf features separates
human text from GPT-3.5 with accuracy above 99% (Desaire et al., 2023), and interpretable
features give AUC 0.988–1.000 under transfer across prompts on news (Vera-Jimenez et al., 2026).
The lexical diversity measures such studies rely on have themselves been
validated: MTLD is the only one of the compared indices independent of text
length (McCarthy and Jarvis, 2010), MATTR solves the same problem with a moving window (Covington and McFall, 2010). The intrinsic
dimensionality of embeddings provides an invariant: about 9 for alphabetic
languages and about 7 for Chinese, with machine text roughly 1.5 lower (Tulchinskii et al., 2023). Grammatical and
rhetorical differences persist as models grow and intensify in
instruction-tuned versions (Reinhart et al., 2025). Formal syntactic parsing distinguishes the
distributions of HPSG grammar types across six models and humans (Zamaraeva et al., 2025). On 225
argumentative texts, the model shows an excess of performed uncertainty
markers: form-meaning divergence is higher in machine texts at p < 0.001 and
Δ = 0.68 (Bakhshi, 2026). The discriminating power also holds on phonological,
morphological and lexical features of essays of equal length (Georgiou, 2025).

Which features are transferable is where studies diverge. A test of 284 features on 27
models and ten domains left a single class stable — measures of lexical
density (El Attar et al., 2026). Studies that selected features within one type of
text report high quality on that same type (Przystalski et al., 2026, Georgiou, 2025) — transferability they did not
test. A separate line: the direction of differences in lexical diversity
changes with the composition of the human control group (Terčon and Dobrovoljc, 2025 versus Fredrick and Craven, 2025).

A recurring design limitation: a feature is validated on class separation, not
on stability across production conditions. A paper reports that the feature
separates human from machine, and almost never what a change of genre,
regimentation or the writer's level of preparation does to it.

For our task the second point matters more. A feature that reverses direction
with the composition of the comparison is unfit for the role of a universal
threshold, and the question of what exactly it measures remains open.

### 3. Hybrid and edited authorship


Human editing breaks detection harder than a change of model. On 6.5 thousand
expert-edited texts, an expert's edit moves machine text away from detection,
while text edited by a model is not recognised as human (Artemova et al., 2025).
Under word-level co-authorship, metric methods yield a mean F1 of 0.462
(Su et al., 2025). On 1445 hybrid CoAuthor texts, the best pairing of segmenter and
classifier gives 0.5166 Kappa, and the authors name the cause: almost half of the
texts have a mean homogeneous span of three sentences or less (Zeng et al., 2024).
Tools that distinguish four categories instead of two, including "machine, then
humanised" and "human, then polished by a model", appeared in response to the
same problem (Abassy et al., 2024). The adjacent task of authorship attribution degrades
on jointly written text compared with purely human baselines:
embeddings are stronger in binary detection, n-gram models are more stable in
verification (Richburg et al., 2024). The volume of the edit itself is measured separately from its result —
LZ77 compression correlates with editing time at 0.81 against 0.59–0.68 for
Levenshtein distance (Devatine and Abraham, 2024).

What editing does to style is where assessments diverge. A preregistered
experiment with 81 participants showed a shift towards the writer's own style (g=0.55) — and
at the same time greater retained closeness to the model than to the same
person's unassisted text (g=−1.43), plus a rise in stylistic homogeneity
(g=1.42) (Baumler et al., 2026). That is, editing simultaneously brings the text closer to its author and
leaves it machine-like by measurement; the conclusion depends on which of the two
quantities is taken as the target.

A recurring design limitation: the mode of editing is varied, but not the
conditions of the original writing. The human reference in these studies is
written freely — the participant writes as they wish, with no brief, no mandatory
sections and no length requirement. CoAuthor records interaction traces (Lee et al., 2022), but not regimentation.

Here the gap is most clearly visible. The literature measures what editing does
to a text, and does not measure what the task does to it.

### 4. Attacks and robustness


Evading a detector is cheap. Substituting homoglyphs for characters drops the mean MCC
of seven systems from 0.64 to −0.01 (Creo and Pudasaini, 2025). Paraphrasing through DIPPER lowers
DetectGPT accuracy from 70.3% to 4.6%, while a defence by retrieval over a base of 15 million generations
recovers 80–97% at FPR 1% on human texts (Krishna et al., 2023). Recursive paraphrasing of
passages of around 300 tokens reduces detection with little loss of quality (Sadasivan et al., 2023).
Stylistic humanisation gives a mean ASR of 92% against five detectors (Gu et al., 2026).
A stress test across four categories — editing, paraphrase, prompting, joint
generation — drops quality by 35%, and no system holds against all of them
(Wang et al., 2024b). Changing a few characters flips the decision (Huang et al., 2024).

Whether this can be fixed is where conclusions diverge. Some studies propose
architectural remedies and show gains of 6.5–18.25% absolute accuracy under attack (Huang et al., 2024),
inference-time adaptation from 24.1% to 90.5% on adversarial material (Ren et al., 2026), or
separation of content and expression (Bao et al., 2025). Others record that with an FPR ceiling of
0.5%, only one of four tested systems retains detection (Jabarian and Imas, 2025).
The disagreement is explained not by method but by the FPR at which quality is measured.

A recurring design limitation: the attack is modelled as a hostile action
after the text is written. Editing to evade and editing for quality are not
separated in these studies, though mechanically they differ: the former targets
the detector, the latter the reader.

Our question lies nearby, but in a different plane. Regimentation, a template
and a length requirement change the text before it is produced, with no intent
whatsoever to deceive a detector, and in the terms of these studies such editing
is not an attack at all.

### 5. Fairness and the cost of a false positive


Detector error is unevenly distributed across groups of writers. Detectors
systematically assign English texts by non-native speakers to the machine class
while classifying native speakers' texts correctly (Liang et al., 2023). Across sixteen systems,
essays by learners of English receive the machine label more often (Stowe et al., 2026). The BAID benchmark measures recall on
texts of under-represented groups across seven categories, including dialect and
formality (Basu et al., 2026). An audit of four detectors on 1992 excerpts from six everyday
genres: open systems mistake up to 78% of human text for machine text, and with
an FPR ceiling of 0.5% only one commercial system retains detection (Jabarian and Imas, 2025).

Assessments of human judgement diverge. Five annotators with daily
LLM experience made one error on 300 English-language non-fiction articles and beat most
detectors (Russell et al., 2025). Participants in a Japanese web survey correctly identified human
text in 31.5% of cases, whereas stylometry on the same material gave 99.8%
(Zaitsu et al., 2025). This is a range of results from different tasks: the language, the genre, the
amount of material presented and the judge's preparation all differ. The overall
conclusion is more modest than either of the two originals — the accuracy of
human judgement about origin depends strongly on the conditions of the
experiment. Separately, Zhu et al., 2024 shows that the "AI" label moves
human evaluation more than the text itself: a machine passage labelled
"human" receives 68.4% preference against 1.537–1.650 in blind comparison.

A recurring design limitation: inequality is sought along attributes of the
writer — language status, demographics, dialect. Writing conditions do not
enter this grid, though formal register and a rigid template are available to
any author regardless of group.

For our work this is the nearest neighbour. If the machine-likeness score rises
with regimentation, then part of the measured subgroup effect may be explained
not by the group but by the conditions under which that group usually writes.

### 6. Evaluation of style transfer and editing


The toolkit for evaluating edits is less developed than the editing itself. A
survey of 97 style transfer studies: human evaluation protocols are
under-specified and not standardised, and reproducibility suffers (Briakou et al., 2021). Only a few automatic
metrics have been validated in experiments with humans (Ostheimer et al., 2023). Content preservation
metrics do not abstract away from the change of style, so meta-evaluation on
existing datasets leads to false conclusions (Pauli et al., 2025). Metrics are tested on sentiment
transfer and detoxification in English, Hindi and Bengali through
correlation with human judgements (Mukherjee et al., 2025).

The very separability of style and content is also contested. Studies of adversarial
encoder-decoder systems conclude that style within them does not separate from
content (Jafaritazehjani et al., 2020). Applied studies nevertheless measure style shift as a separate
quantity and obtain meaningful numbers: BERTScore F1 0.924 with 17 times fewer
parameters (Paneru, 2026).

A recurring design limitation: the target style is defined as a class — polite,
formal, non-toxic. Style as a consequence of an external requirement — a
template, a mandatory set of sections, a length limit — is not considered in
these settings.

Hence a methodological foothold for our work and at the same time a caution:
style shift can be measured, but the metric must be chosen with an eye to the
fact that its validity has not been tested everywhere.

### 7. What of this has been tested on Russian


Beyond English the picture differs. On Portuguese news, a disinformation
detection model gives 93% accuracy on human texts and 75% on
LLM outputs, and confidence markers in ChatGPT's factual news are 152% higher (Rodrigues et al., 2026). On
Japanese public comments, stylometry separates the classes with 99.8% accuracy
(Zaitsu et al., 2025). Multilingual shared tasks and benchmarks cover from 11 to 22 languages
(Wang et al., 2024c, Macko et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025, Macko et al., 2023).

The sample contains five primary Russian-language studies. RuATD 2022 assembled 14 generators
and put binary and multi-class detection into a shared task, with 30 systems in the binary
subtask (Shamardina et al., 2022). LLMTrace annotates mixed authorship character by character for
English and Russian and supports locating machine spans within a text
(Tolstykh et al., 2025). AINL-Eval 2025 provided 52 305 Russian scientific abstracts from twelve
fields, five generators, and a requirement of transfer to unseen fields and models
(Batura et al., 2025). Russian is also part of multilingual benchmarks (Macko et al., 2023).

The fourth study stands closest to the subject of the present research. CoAT is
a corpus of 246 thousand human and artificial texts, thirteen generators,
six domains from the Russian National Corpus to digital diaries (Shamardina et al., 2024).
A transformer detector achieves accuracy 0.864 there, humans on the same data — 0.66,
generator attribution among fourteen candidates — macro-F1 0.521. Transfer to
unseen models and domains does not hold.

What matters for us is another dimension of the same study: **detectability
depends strongly on the type of the generative task.** Machine-translated texts
are recognised with accuracy 0.621, paraphrase — 0.768, free generation — 0.99.
That is, the way a text was produced shifts recognition accuracy from 0.621
to 0.99.

CoAT stops in the same place as the rest. The generator changes together with
the task type, so the effect of production conditions is not separated from the
effect of origin. The human part is collected from the same six domains with no
control of task, regimentation or channel. The authors name the limitations
themselves: no diversity of decoding strategies and a risk of corpus obsolescence.

The fifth study comes from the practice of document checking. Gritsay and
co-authors address the detection of machine fragments within scientific and
educational texts and bring quality to F1 0.968 for Russian at precision 0.970
and recall 0.968; the same approach gives 0.825 for English (Gritsay et al., 2023). The quality rests on multi-domain training and
paraphrase augmentation, and false positives are controlled with the
Benjamini-Hochberg correction. The authors' conclusion matches the line of the
whole section: a universal detector for all domains and languages, in their
estimate, is hardly achievable.

The divergence here is not between results but between the scope of the tasks.
Russian material is covered along the line of text origin and noticeably less
so along the line of the conditions of its creation: none of the five studies
varies task mode, regimentation or channel while origin stays the same.

The recurring design limitation is the same as in section 1, only sharper: the
corpus is taken from available sources, and the writing conditions of the human
part are not documented.

**Among the included studies, no FPR estimates were found simultaneously for
formal and for translated Russian-language prose.**

### Summary: what remains unmeasured


The literature measures two things with confidence. The first is the origin of
a text, and here both the methods and the limits of their transfer are known.
The second is the result of editing, and here it is known that expert editing
moves a text away from detection, while style after it stays closer to the
model than to the author.

**In the included sample of 73 studies we found no research that separately
assessed how text production conditions — task mode, regimentation level and
generation channel — change a text's machine-likeness indicators while its
origin stays the same.**

Three groups of studies come right up to this point and stop. Prevalence
estimates count the share of text that has passed through a model: from 6.5% to 16.9%
in the reviews of four conferences (Liang et al., 2024), at least 13.5% of biomedical abstracts
in 2024 (Kobak et al., 2025). Studies of hybrid authorship vary the mode of editing but not the
conditions of the original writing (Artemova et al., 2025, Su et al., 2025, Zeng et al., 2024, Baumler et al., 2026). Fairness studies measure
the dependence of the score on the writer's group, not on the conditions under which the writer wrote (Liang et al., 2023,
Stowe et al., 2026, Basu et al., 2026).

The practical stakes are visible from Fredrick and Craven, 2025: the direction of differences in lexical
diversity changes with the composition of the human control group. If a
machine-likeness indicator depends on the conditions under which the human
reference was written, a threshold calibrated on one corpus transfers to
another without guarantees. In accusatory use this means the risk of punishing
regimentation rather than the origin of the text.

Add to this Geng and Trotta, 2025: the frequency of words publicly named as characteristic of the model
falls in arXiv abstracts soon after such lists are published — authors adapt.
Any conclusion that rests on a marker dictionary has a shelf life, and this is
an argument for features not reducible to a word list.

# Methods

### 1. Corpus


The analytical corpus contains 1882 Russian-language documents: 1079 machine and
803 human. Its composition is fixed by the registry `04-corpus/documents-registry.csv`;
each document carries a passport of 60 fields, including generation channel, task mode,
genre, publication date, licence status and split groups.

#### 1.0. Data flow


Corpus composition by stage and stratum is given in Table S1.

| Stage | Machine | Human | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before extraction correction and final exclusions | 1079 | 837 | 1916 |
| Excluded after correction | 0 | 34 | 34 |
| Analytical corpus `prep-v5` | 1079 | 803 | 1882 |

All 34 exclusions fell on the human subset and on a single cause: after

#### 1.1. Machine subset


The machine subset is built to the design `45 tasks × 4 generation channels × 3 task modes × 2 replicates`. The forty-five tasks are distributed evenly across three
genres — analytics, commercial text, SEO. The task modes: **P1** — a neutral
technical brief, **P2** — a strict editorial brief, **P3** — a task requiring the
avoidance of typical machine-text features. The full design yields 1080 cells;
the corpus has 1079, because one `prompt_condition=P3` cell was missing in the
analytics genre.

**The unit of variation is the generation channel, not the model family.** A channel
comprises the model, the access wrapper and the wrapper's potentially hidden system
prompt. These cannot be separated: for two channels the wrapper adds its own system
prompt to the request, and its content cannot be reconstructed after the fact.
Between-channel differences therefore cannot be attributed to the model. Channels,
models and access parameters are given in Table S2.

| Channel | Model | Wrapper and access | Open weights | Temperature and top-p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `real_claude` | `claude-opus-4-8` | nested CLI, clean process per cell | no | channel defaults |
| `gpt` | `gpt-5.5` | `codex exec`, reasoning effort high | no | channel defaults |
| `deepseek_pro` | `deepseek-v4-pro` | HTTPS API without a wrapper | yes | 1.0 / 1.0 |
| `nemotron` | `nemotron-3-ultra-free` | HTTPS API via a gateway | yes | 1.0 / 1.0 |

The channels were chosen so that the models belong to different families rather
than to versions of one platform. Each channel produced 270 documents, except
`nemotron` with 269.

Two limitations were recorded before collection. First: published weights for a
family do not prove that a hosted response came from a known checkpoint with known
quantisation, so these channels cannot be called reproducible. Second: the seed
`random.Random(20260723)` fixes the order of cells, not the model output —
reproducibility means repeatability of the procedure, not byte-level identity of
the texts.

Two channels operate through a CLI and carry a residual wrapper system prompt that
cannot be removed; it is declared part of the channel. The wrapper version is not
constant within a channel: the OpenAI channel has two recorded CLI versions, and
for Anthropic the version was not recorded at all. The content of the system
prompts cannot be reconstructed after the fact, so one cannot claim that the
prompt changed — nor the opposite.

A response is described by two independent variables instead of a single validity
threshold. `technical_validity` covers response integrity: below 50 words a
response counts as a technical failure and is regenerated.
`length_instruction_compliance` describes compliance with the instruction on a
length of 1200–1800 words in five grades from `severe underlength` to `severe
overlength`. The separation was introduced because the previous single threshold
of 480 words systematically sent P3-mode texts for regeneration and would thereby
have biased the primary outcome. A model refusal is not screened out by the
threshold and remains a result.

### 2. Identifiability and outcome status


Design identifiability was checked on 27 July 2026 against the registry passports,
**before the dependent variable was computed** (`design-support-table.md`).

The check showed that genre in the human subset serves as a proxy for both the
regimentation level and origin. Level 3 is represented by the single genre
`science`, level 0 by the single genre `prose`; for the groups of the primary
outcome O3 the genre intersection is empty.

Hence the statuses assigned before computation:

- **O1** — a pre-specified contrast. The difference between modes P2 and P1 lives
  inside the machine subset, where task, genre and channel are fully crossed, and
  the contrast itself was registered before data collection.
- **O2 and O3** — descriptive. The registered estimates for them are not computed,
  and no p-values are published.

**The study is not a fully confirmatory analysis.** The O1 contrast was specified
in advance, but three of the four operationalisations used to estimate it received
their operationalisation on 27 July, after the results of the first procedure had
been seen (§4). The correct formulation: a pre-specified contrast estimated
predominantly with exploratory operationalisations. Confirmatory status belongs to
the question, not to the whole set of measurement instruments.

The downgrade of O2 and O3 took place before the machine-likeness score was
computed and does not depend on the obtained numbers.

### 4. Four operationalisations of the machine-likeness score


The effect is tested in four ways so that the conclusion does not reduce to an
artefact of a single implementation. No procedure is designated the reference:
sign agreement across all four is an argument, divergence is a result in its own
right.

1. **Interpretable index.** A weighted procedure over countable features, with
   weights fixed before data collection. Scale 0–100.
2. **Trained classifier.** Logistic regression on the primary features, evaluated
   only on group holdouts by topic, source, model, task mode, genre, revision
   family and time. Nested cross-validation for regularisation; the diagnostic
   baselines "topic only" and "source only" are mandatory.
3. **Zero-shot metric.** Mean NLL under an open Russian-language model.
4. **Judge model.** The median of three runs with fixed seeds; the dispersion is
   recorded separately.

Procedures 2–4 carry exploratory status: their operationalisation was written on
27 July, after the results of procedure 1 had been seen, whereas the
preregistration specified each in a single line. Procedures 1 and 2 count as one
family of evidence: both aggregate the same feature matrix over the same documents
and differ in how the weights are obtained.

### 5. Estimation of the primary outcome O1


O1 is the difference in machine-likeness score between task modes on paired
`task × model × replicate` observations; two contrasts are registered, `P3 − P1`
and `P2 − P1`. The unit is points on the 0–100 scale.

The cluster is the task: forty-five tasks, each containing all channels, modes and
replicates. Confidence intervals are computed by cluster bootstrap over tasks
at the 95% level; the absolute difference with its interval is published,
not only a p-value.

The family of primary outcomes is closed by a Bonferroni correction for the two
registered tests, α = 0.025 per test. Sensitivity contrasts are compared against
the same threshold.

Benjamini–Hochberg was registered for the feature family and receives the status
**not applicable**: no inferential tests of individual features were run, and the
four differently scaled summary contrasts do not form a homogeneous family. The
wording was approved by the PI before computation.

Individual features in the figures and the error analysis remain descriptive:
effects and intervals without selection by p-value.

### 6. Mixed-effects: three models and the block outcome


The preregistration specifies three models (Table 8; identifiability diagnostics
in Table S8):

| Model | Corpus | Random effects | Yields |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | machine | task, replicate | O1 |
| M2 | human | source, author, task | O2 |
| M3 | combined | task, source, author, channel | O3 |

The order of execution was fixed before fitting: first an identifiability check
against the design — crossing of factor levels, number of clusters per random
effect, degeneracy of interactions — then fitting only the identifiable models.
On convergence failure the diagnostics are saved in full: optimiser warnings,
eigenvalues, the simplified specification.

None of the three models yielded interpretable estimates. M1 did not converge
after removal of an exact linear dependency and exhaustion of the standard
BFGS–L-BFGS–CG chain. M2 proved structurally non-identifiable. For M3 there was no
support for the registered cross-genre origin estimand: within the SEO genre a
conditional contrast is technically available, but it does not identify the origin
effect for the whole corpus.

The wording for the paper was approved by the PI: the estimates were not obtained
or lacked the required interpretation. Claiming that the mixed-effects models
"found no effect" is prohibited — that is a different claim.

Switching the optimiser to Powell or Nelder–Mead, simplifying the random effects,
or a separate model for SEO after the block was closed would have constituted a
new exploratory analysis and were not performed.

The wild cluster bootstrap does not replace these models: it assesses the
stability of the mean paired contrast, whereas mixed-effects models capture the
nesting and the heterogeneity across tasks and channels. Substituting one for the
other in the text is not permitted.

### 7. Stability stress test


The panel comprises 60 documents selected and fixed before the run
(`07-analysis/stress-panel-v1.csv`). The panel does not change between revisions.

Of the sixteen registered transformations, ten are executed: em-dash replacement,
markup changes, heading removal, paragraph reordering, punctuation correction,
text shortening, text expansion, adding spelling errors, homoglyph substitution,
transfer between formats. Six are not assessed: five require material absent from
the corpus — the A→H branch with editors, machine rewriting, translation and
back-translation, paraphrasing; the sixth, removal of typical clichés, was ruled
infeasible after `prep-v5`, because the registered artefact list produces no hits
on the cleaned text.

The panel of 60 documents and 10 transformations yields 600 primary
`document × transformation` cells. Each cell passes through all four procedures on
their native scales: the index on 0–100, the classifier as the probability of
class A, NLL in its own units, the judge on 0–100 with the median of three
seeds.

**The instability threshold is set separately for each procedure, before the run.**
A single threshold cannot be applied: the procedures operate on different scales.
Inputs, scales, directions and thresholds of the four procedures are summarised in
Table 2.

The notation is kept distinct: **P1, P2 and P3 are task modes** (§1.1); the
procedures are referred to by number.

<!-- ТАБЛИЦА 2: tab-02-four-operationalisations -->
| Procedure | Scale | Instability event rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1, style index | 0–100 | `\|Δ\| > 5.0` points; exactly 5.0 does not count as an event |
| 4, judge | 0–100 | `\|Δ\| > 5.0` points on the median of three seeds |
| 2, classifier | probability | instability: `\|Δp\| > 0.05`; decision flip: crossing the 0.5 threshold — a separate quantity |
| 3, NLL | its own | no binary threshold is applied; continuous `ΔNLL` is published |

For procedure 2 the two quantities are computed separately, following the
aggregation rule of the amendment: the share of eligible models with
`|Δp| > 0.05` is the instability, the share crossing the decision threshold is
the decision flip; both are averaged over models within a document and then over
the 60 documents with equal weight. For procedure 3 the absolute change cannot be
called points, so no share of unstable cases is defined for it at all.

The 5.0 threshold is operational, not empirically validated. Alongside the event
share, continuous shift magnitudes are published — median, IQR, 95th percentile,
maximum — for all four procedures.

The percentile of a transformed text is computed against the unchanged genre pool:
transformed values are not added to the pool, and the document's original value is
not removed from it.

### 8. Versioning and invalidation rules


Each run is described by a manifest with hashes of code, inputs and outputs. An
erroneous manifest is not deleted: it is marked `invalidated` with a reason, and
work continues in a new revision. A revision voids the child manifests assembled
under the previous parent and forces their procedures to be recomputed. The final
numbers of the main series were obtained under the sixth revision in a single
chain; the stress test stands at revision r5 after three invalidated ones.

Changes to the computation code require an amendment and a new revision. Hashes of
code and inputs are fixed before launch; the chain verifies them and blocks the
start on any mismatch. Code checking takes place before the run, on synthetic data
of the same type that production supplies.

Parse caches are keyed by `document_id × input_sha256 × cache_version ×
model_revision`. The preprocessing version is recorded as provenance but is not
part of the validity check: a byte-identical input yields a byte-identical parse.
New entries do not overwrite earlier ones, so earlier series remain reproducible.

The procedure completion gate checks completeness mechanically — the number of
cells, feature coverage, denominators, absence of dropped categories — and stops
the chain on a `blocked` or `incomplete` status. A triggered gate counts as a
quality control result; loosening the tolerance to let a run pass is prohibited.

A separate invariant checks cells whose input has not changed: when the `prose`
profile, the `full` profile and all preprocessing counters match, the score must
equal the baseline identically, with a tolerance of 1e-9.

# Results

### 1. Corpus composition and QA closure


The corpus comprises 1882 documents: 1079 machine and 803 human (Table 1; the
data flow and per-stratum exclusions are in Table S1). The machine part was
produced by four generation channels — each backed by its own model family — across 45 tasks in three task modes with two
replicates. The human part consists of archival texts published before
2022-01-01. The active preprocessing version is `prep-v5`; the features are
collected in `feature-matrix-v5.csv`.

Preprocessing removes collection artefacts, and most of its rules apply to the
human part: comment threads in 67 documents totalling 680 570 characters,
sidebars and navigation, dangling lead-ins, paragraph segmentation in 304
documents, typographic hyphenation of the form `за- ключение` — 6955 cases in
124 documents, all human: `urfu` 3460, `spbgu` 3390, `cyberleninka` 100, with
isolated cases in `taiga_magazines`, `taiga_arzamas` and `lenta`. For the
machine part, the channel wrapper was removed: 99 `deepseek_pro` documents
opened with an address to the client who commissioned the text instead of the article text.
The asymmetry of the rules is acknowledged and recorded: syllable hyphenation is
produced by PDF layout, while machine texts arrive from the API without layout.

Residual verbatim repeats were measured by a symmetric scan of both parts. The
threshold is a share of repeated sentences above 0.10 with a length filter of
40 characters. **17 human documents exceeded the threshold, and none of the
1079 machine documents did**; in the machine part the share of repeats is zero
for every document, not merely below the threshold. On the previous
preprocessing version the same ratio was sharper: 97 against zero, so the
correction removed about eighty per cent of the cases.

The seventeen candidates were classified manually by a single rater (Table
S9): 10 are confirmed extraction defects, 4 are legitimate properties of the
source, 3 remain unresolved. The defects are block-level repeats of fragments
in the intermediary dataset `buriy_2014` and a cookie-plugin line. The
legitimate repeats are a doubled bibliography in three SPbU papers, where the
`References` section splits the repeats exactly in half, and a block of
interview questions. Separately, a mechanism common to the preprocessing is
recorded: detaching a heading from text fused to it can produce a verbatim
repeat where the source had none.

fully prospective registration of all four results: by the time the plan was
fixed, the result of the first procedure was already known.

both aggregate the same matrix over the same documents.

### 3. Index decomposition, classifier ablation, and comparison of run schemes


These are three different things, and they must not be conflated. The first is
an algebraic decomposition of the index contrast, exact by construction. The
second is a comparison of separately trained models, which is not a
decomposition. The third is a comparison of two frozen runs with each other.

#### 3.1. Index decomposition: algebraic and exact


The index is assembled from categories with weights fixed before data
collection, so its contrast decomposes into summands exactly; the check of the
decomposition on the v1 series is in Table S14. Both categories are computed
for all documents; the denominator is the same.

| Component of the `P3 − P1` contrast | Value |
|---|---|
| contribution of the style categories (common) | **+1.6740** |
| contribution of the structural category (format) | **−3.4395** |
| sum of contributions | −1.7655 |
| full contrast of the frozen run | −1.7655 |

The discrepancy between the sum and the full contrast is 1.6·10⁻⁴ — rounding
to four digits when writing to CSV.

The mechanism reads off directly: the common component shifted the index
upward by +1.6740 points, while the format component shifted it downward by
−3.4395 and determined the negative overall contrast. The format contribution
exceeds the common contribution roughly twofold in absolute value.

This is a property of this index on this corpus, not a universal ratio: the
category weights are set by the specification, and the feature set fully
covers two categories out of thirteen. The index without the structural
category gives +2.0264 [0.8953; 3.1429], p = 0.0016 — the sign flips.

#### 3.2. Classifier: ablation, not decomposition


The `full` and `net` models are trained separately, so their difference cannot
be called a contribution of structure. Both contrasts appear in Table 4
alongside the quality of the model variants.

<!-- ТАБЛИЦА 4: tab-04-classifier-and-ablation -->
| Model variant | `P3 − P1` contrast |
|---|---|
| full model | −0.0941, p = 0.0028 |
| without the four structural features | +0.0169 [−0.0134; 0.0496], p = 0.3168 |

The correct wording: excluding the structural features changed the direction

#### 3.3. Comparing the two runs with each other


A separate question is what each of the two changes between the frozen runs
contributed. Both changed the selected regularisation for three holdouts out
of eighteen.

**Corpus correction** — 34 documents were excluded, and the text changed in
68. The shifts are small in absolute value on average and concentrated on one
holdout: AUROC 0.005 on average with a maximum of +0.034, MCC 0.029 with
+0.093, FPR 0.021 with −0.078. The maximum across all six metrics falls on
`holdout_genre_seo`.

**Nested CV fix** — the corpus is the same; only the inner-split scheme
changed: for seven holdouts the validation became two-class. The shifts are of
the same order but with the opposite sign for SEO: AUROC −0.050, MCC −0.227,
FPR +0.234, TPR@1%FPR −0.869.

The wording of the last result is fixed and matters: the degenerate fold
happened to select a stronger regularisation, which worked better on this
outer holdout, and this can only be learned from the test metrics. Writing
that it "picked C more successfully" is prohibited — such a wording would
present a defect as an advantage.

The defect predates the correction: seven of the 54 planned inner folds had a
single-class validation already in the first version of the classifier, where
the selection of C skipped them silently — neither the metrics, nor the
manifest, nor the report contained the number of folds used.

### 6. Mixed-effects: three different reasons for the absence of estimates


None of the three registered models produced interpretable estimates, and the
reasons differ.

**M1** — the machine corpus, yields O1. The matrix was reduced to full rank 22
of 22, but convergence was not reached by any of the three optimisers
permitted in advance: BFGS, L-BFGS and CG, with gradient norms 35.26, 15.62
and 4.47. Coefficients and intervals were not interpreted. The O1 conclusions
rest on paired within-task contrasts and a cluster bootstrap over the 45
tasks.

**M2** — the human corpus. Structurally unidentifiable: rank 7 of 22, all
twelve `level × genre` interactions are degenerate, and the registered random
term "task" has zero clusters in the human part.

**M3** — the cross-genre estimand of origin. The registered contrast has no
support in the data.

The wording "mixed-effects found no effect" is wrong: the estimates were not
obtained or lacked the required interpretation, which is a different claim.
The bootstrap is the basis of the current estimates but not an equivalent
repair of the non-converged model: it assesses the stability of the mean
paired contrast, whereas mixed-effects models the nesting and the
heterogeneity across tasks and channels.

Powell, Nelder–Mead, simplification of the random effects and a separate model
for SEO would, after the closure of the block, have constituted a new
exploratory analysis and were not run.

*Sources: `07-analysis/mixed-m1-v2.md`, `mixed-identifiability.md`,
`analysis-closure.md` §1.*

### 8. Manual analysis of unstable cases


Thirty cases were selected by a rule fixed before selection: sorting by the share of
models that flipped their decision, with ties broken by the largest probability shift.
The set is 30 cells on 28 documents, with a cutoff share of 0.750.

The analysis was blind; the procedure was structural attribution. Hidden were the document identifier, the origin class,
the split, the transformation name, both probabilities, the direction of the decision
flip and the position in the selection; two texts were presented in random order. The
verdict table was fixed by hash before the key was revealed.

**The procedure is called blind structural attribution with programmatic text
matching**, not manual reading, and this matters for reading the result. Pairs
were matched programmatically — number of sentences, paragraph separators,
repeated occurrences, the set of unique sentences and a line-by-line diff — and the
verbatim fragments cited as grounds were extracted from the differences found. The
analysis took two minutes forty-four seconds with a single rater. This procedure
establishes the mechanism of change and makes no claim to semantic expertise: shifts
not visible in structure it does not detect.

#### Correspondence of verdicts to transformations


Verdicts for each of the thirty cards are given in Table S10.

as a difference in spaces before punctuation marks with the sentence and paragraph
composition unchanged.

Distribution by origin class: all three interpretable cases are human documents;
twenty-five of the twenty-six composite ones are machine.

#### The case found precisely because of blinding


The card with transformation t02 received the verdict "indeterminate" on the ground
that "the texts match character for character". This turned out to be a correct
observation: for document `nemotron_b016_P1_r1`, the `full` profile before and after
the transformation is identical — 14 485 characters, a byte-for-byte match. Yet
`flip_rate` = 1.000 and max\|Δp\| = 0.9664: the probability of machine origin falls
from 0.97–1.00 to 0.03–0.23 across all six eligible models.

The result, stated: **removing emphasis markers did not change the `full` profile
by a single character, but it changed the distribution of the text between headings
and `prose`, the values of the format counters and the classifier's input vector. In
all six applicable models this turned a confident machine decision into a human
one.**

The mechanism can be traced through the files. The profiles contain no markdown
at all — preprocessing strips the markup. The transformation changes it in the source
text, and this shifts the boundary between heading and prose: lines like "Как перевести
„сеошные“ отчеты в деньги:", previously recognised as bold headings, cease to be such
and move into the prose profile. `prose` grows from 5540 to
5792 characters, the bold-fragment counter falls from 76 to zero, and the full text
does not change.

The unchanged-input invariant worked correctly: the cell is not flagged
`input_unchanged`, because the markup counters changed.

This should be read as sensitivity of the entire measurement pipeline, not as a
direct effect of bold type. The model reacts not only to the words of the text but
also to the preprocessing decisions about which fragments count as prose: the
prose-profile features receive different input, and the format features see zero
emphasis spans instead of seventy-six.

#### Limitations of the procedure


**The card showed the `full` profile.** Transformations acting through the
`prose`/`full` boundary and through the markup counters are entirely invisible
in it — which is exactly why the t02 case received the verdict "indeterminate". For
this class of effects, blind analysis on a single profile is insensitive.

**Texts were truncated to 4000 characters.** Truncation equalises volume: in all
thirty cards both texts run to 494–589 words, and the growth in length under expansion
is not visible in the card. Identification of t11 relied on duplication and paragraphs,
not on volume.

**The composition of the set limits the blinding.** Twenty-six of the thirty cases
fall on a single transformation, so after a few cards the type of impact reconstructs
itself. The limitation was recorded before the analysis began.
A separate test for an order effect was not introduced: it had not been registered.

**Two cards were relabelled before the table was fixed.** An automatic heuristic
attributed them to a difference in spaces, whereas what is there is movement of blocks;
this was corrected by observation, without opening the key. Both belong to t04, and
after the correction the verdict matched the mechanism.

# Discussion

### 1. There is no single measurable "machine-likeness"


Four procedures assess one corpus on the same documents and disagree in sign.
On the `P3 − P1` contrast, the style index, the classifier and zero-shot NLL give
a minus, the judge model a plus, and none of the four intervals covers zero. On
the second contrast the signs of procedures 1, 2 and 4 diverge the same way;
there the diverging procedure 3 is indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.1988), so
its contribution to the disagreement is weaker.

This is not an error in one of the procedures that should be found and fixed.
Their inputs differ: two work with the feature matrix, two with raw text; one has
weights fixed before data collection, another has weights trained on the data.
The sign disagreement is a property of the construct, not a measurement failure.

The direct practical consequence: the question "which detector is correct" is
ill-posed. The better question is what exactly each of them operationalises, and
whether their operationalisations coincide in the application of interest.

They cannot be averaged. Index points, class probability, mean token NLL and a
rubric scale are four different scales, and a pooled effect across them is
meaningless. Procedures 1 and 2 are also not independent: both aggregate the same
feature matrix.

### 2. Corpus assembly conditions are stronger than stylistic signals


The baseline on a single input — the source identifier — reached a median AUROC
of 1.0000 across the thirteen holdouts where AUROC is defined, above the model on twenty-two style features
(0.9973). For comparison: the length baseline gives 0.4364, the negative control
with cluster-level shuffled labels 0.3614.

The source identifier turned out to be an almost deterministic proxy for the
origin class. The corpus itself is built that way: human texts were collected
from platforms, machine texts were produced by generation channels, and no source
supplies both classes.

The practical limit this imposes is hard. The assessment does not transfer to
sources where human and machine texts coexist — and that is exactly the situation
detectors are applied to: one platform, one section, a mixed stream. High metrics
on group holdouts do not justify such transfer.

### 3. The classifier learns corpus mechanisms, not only style


The stress test produced a chain in which every link is measured.

Verbatim sentence repeats in the corpus occur only in human texts: 17 human
documents exceeded the 0.10 threshold and none of the 1079 machine documents did,
and in the machine part the repeat share is zero for every document. Of the
seventeen candidates, ten turned out to be extraction defects, four were
legitimate properties of the source, and three remained unresolved.

Feature M01 — the standard deviation of cosine similarity between adjacent
sentences — is systematically higher in human texts: median 0.0907 versus 0.0746,
Cliff's δ = −0.78. Its coefficient is negative in all eighteen models, with a
median rank of 4 out of 22 by absolute value.

Sentence duplication raises this quantity by 7.86 σ and shifts the decision
towards the "human" class. All 109 decision flips under text expansion go in one
direction.

The mechanism is corpus- and model-specific. It explains the behaviour of this
classifier on this corpus and does not make verbatim repetition a marker of human
writing: the association arose because repeats in the training data came from one
side, mostly as a trace of collection.

### 4. Preprocessing is part of the measurement instrument


The `nemotron_b016_P1_r1` case shows this in pure form. Removing emphasis markers
did not change the `full` profile by a single character — 14 485 characters
byte-for-byte — but changed the distribution of text between headings and
`prose`, the values of the format counters and the classifier's input vector. In
all six applicable models, a confident machine decision flipped to human: the
probability dropped from 0.97–1.00 to 0.03–0.23.

The mechanism is traceable: lines previously recognised as bold headings stop
being headings and move into the prose profile. `prose` grows from 5540 to 5792
characters, the emphasis counter falls from 76 to zero.

So the model reacts not only to the words of the text but also to preprocessing
decisions about which fragments count as prose. The data preparation pipeline
enters the measurement instrument on a par with the model, and its version must
be published and pinned the same way as the weights.

The case was found by blind structural attribution: the card showed the `full`
profile, where there are no differences, and the verdict "the intervention showed
no effect" was a correct observation alongside a decision flip of 0.97.

### 5. Formal register produces a systematic bias, but this is not demographic unfairness


The registered subgroup disparity rule fired for the `HH-formal-register`
subgroup in 5 of 18 holdouts: the subgroup's median FPR is 0.066, with a
difference of 0.043 from the rest of the test set. The aggregate fairness verdict
did not change, but the effect stopped being a property of a single platform.

Calling this demographic unfairness is wrong, and the reason is not caution of
wording. The subgroup coincides with the `science` genre document for document —
167 texts — so register and genre are not separable on this corpus. The group is
defined by text type, not by a property of people.

This coincidence is not isolated. All three hard-human labels reproduce a genre
in full: `HH-formal-register` — 167 `science` texts, `HH-polished` — 152 `news`,
`HH-translation` — 73 `translation`. The annotation was conceived as a layer on
top of the corpus, but in fact it repeated the corpus's genre partition, and
separate estimation of register and genre effects on these data is impossible in
principle, not for lack of power. Separating them requires a corpus where the
formal register occurs in several genres — no such sample exists in this study.

The correct name is an error asymmetry by register. It means that the cost of
error is borne by authors of formal scientific texts, but it does not permit
claims about groups of people.

### 6. Good averages hide genre heterogeneity


Median AUROC of the main model 0.9973, median MCC 0.9338, median FPR 0.0380 — by
average metrics the classifier looks almost error-free.

The spread says otherwise. For `holdout_genre_seo`, AUROC is 0.9433, MCC 0.6560,
FPR 0.3098, TPR@5%FPR 0.6250 against the median 0.9833. The same holdout is the
worst in calibration: Brier 0.1796, ECE 0.1973, MCE 0.7548.

The coincidence is not accidental and it matters: SEO is problematic in
separation quality and in calibration at once. Publishing one averaged figure for
such a profile means hiding that in one genre holdout out of seven every third
human text receives a machine verdict.

The genre label also hides the spread between platforms within a genre: in the
`news` genre the median FPR is 0.013, while across the platforms
inside it — 0.033, 0.000 and 0.000.

### 7. Abstaining from low-confidence decisions reduces risk, but no operating point has been chosen


The risk–coverage curve shows the expected: error falls from 0.0468 at full
coverage to 0.0059 at half coverage. Abstaining from the least confident
decisions works.

The threshold, however, has not been chosen deliberately. There is no justified
number, and setting one after seeing the results would mean fitting it to the
answer. The curve describes the system's behaviour and is not a deployment
recommendation.

Separately: the abstention policy registered for the instrument is formulated in
terms of properties of the input text — length, genre, conflicting signals — not
the predicted probability. The measured coverage does not account for these
abstentions.

Overall calibration is, at that, acceptable — macro Brier 0.0365, ECE robust to
the number of bins in the range 0.0557–0.0581 — but stability of the estimate is
not proof of good calibration, and the heterogeneity from point 6 does not go
away.

### 8. Practical conclusion


The detector assesses a combination of text, source, formatting and pipeline, not
biological or creative authorship directly.

The study establishes this through four measured facts: the source separates the
classes better than style (point 2), the classifier learned the corpus trace of
repeats (point 3), a change of formatting without a change of text flips the
decision (point 4), quality and calibration depend sharply on genre (point 6).

For application this means the following. A "machine text" verdict is not a claim
about authorship and cannot serve as grounds for accusation. An assessment
obtained on one corpus does not transfer to a stream where both classes come from
one source. A change of publication template or of the text preparation pipeline
changes the verdict without the author's involvement.

None of these conclusions requires assuming that detectors are useless. They
require assuming that a detector measures text production conditions together
with the text itself — which was the study's original hypothesis.

### 9. Limitations and future validation


The main limitation is named in point 2: **the classes share no sources.**
Because sources do not overlap between classes, the separate effects of text
origin and platform are not identifiable on these data without additional
untestable assumptions. Future work must include sources that supply both
classes.

**Transformations must be validated on a separate development panel.** The
attempt to register shortening without duplication showed why: the transformation
was rejected at preflight by a criterion that itself proved ill-matched to the
nature of the quantity, and choosing a threshold after seeing the result is
inadmissible. The panel for validating criteria must be separated from the panel
on which the result is computed.

**An external corpus is mandatory.** All conclusions were obtained on one corpus
of one language, collected by one procedure. Transfer to another language,
another domain or another generation of models has not been tested.

**The operationalisation of preprocessing must be stable and published.** Point 4
shows that the decision about the boundary between heading and prose changes the
verdict. Hence the pipeline version is part of the method description, and
comparing detectors without pinning the preprocessing is invalid.

In addition, the known and recorded limitations remain: mixed-effects models did
not yield interpretable estimates for three different reasons, the volume
transformations turned out to be composite, three repeat cases remained
unresolved, and candidate classification was carried out by a single rater.

#### Construct and design


1. **The index covers a small part of the stated construct.** Of thirteen categories, two are fully operationalised, five partially, four are not operationalised, one is reported as a separate output, one is absent. The total weight of the non-operationalised categories in the "Mixed" domain is 0.23 out of 1.00, and it has not been redistributed among the rest. All four share one reason: the source formulates the mechanism as a narrowing of dispersion, while the features measure level. Source: `construct-coverage.md`.
2. **Two of the three primary outcomes are unidentifiable.** In the human part `regulation_level` coincides with `genre` one to one; for the O3 groups the genre intersection is empty. O2 and O3 were reclassified as descriptive before the dependent variable was computed; no p-values were calculated for them. Source: `design-support-table.md`.
3. **Procedures 2–4 have exploratory status.** Their operationalisation was written on 27 July, after the results of procedure 1 had been seen; §7 of the preregistration specified each in a single line. Source: `procedures-2-4-registration.md` §2.
4. **Procedures 1 and 2 are one family of evidence.** Both aggregate the same feature matrix on the same documents and differ in how the weights are obtained. Their agreement counts as one source, not two.
5. **All four procedures share one corpus.** These are alternative measurements on the same documents, not independent samples: shared selection biases, genres, time slice and collection defects. Genuine replication would require a bridge set; its requirements are in `design-support-table.md` §5.
6. **The net estimand cannot be operationalised for procedures 3 and 4.** Removing markup is not the same as subtracting the structure prescribed by the brief: the brief also dictates the order of exposition, the set of sections and the mandatory points. No imitation was created for the sake of table symmetry.

#### Magnitude and precision


7. **All effects are small.** Standardised paired effects of procedure 1: |d_z| from 0.143 to 0.305. The mean differences −1.77 and +3.28 are points of the operationalised index, not percentages of machine-likeness.
8. **The judge's resolution is below the effect size.** 15 unique values of the three-run median across 1882 documents, 85.9% of scores at two points of the scale, ICC(2,1) = 0.477, mean range across three runs 4.78 against an SD of 3.26 for the median machine-part scores.
9. **The intervals are unadjusted, 95%.** Bootstrap replicates were not saved; 97.5% intervals cannot be extracted from the run. Decisions are made by p against α = 0.025 after Bonferroni. The sensitivity contrast O1-net `P2 − P1` does not pass the threshold (p = 0.0416) and is not called significant.
10. **The 120 classifier pairs are 15 task clusters.** The P2b contrast was computed on a different population than the rest: only the seo genre against 45 clusters for procedures 1, 3 and 4.
11. **Four machine source clusters give a weak estimate of cross-channel generalisability** — this limitation is published at every mention of the external P2b folds.

#### Diagnostics that did not work


12. **A cluster-level negative control is not feasible in this design.** Four machine clusters against 124 human ones give a bimodal distribution over twenty permutations (mean AUROC 0.132–0.434 with a spread of 0.001–0.998); the mean is not a null hypothesis. Replacing the cluster permutation with a document permutation would bring the leakage back. The diagnostic is recorded as not feasible.
13. **Five of the eighteen holdouts yield a single-class test**, where only the FPR is defined. The test sets `genre_news`, `genre_science` and `genre_translation` coincide in composition — the same 392 documents of series v2, verified by comparing identifiers — because rule §5.2.1 always keeps hard-human out of training. The three table rows read as one observation; the wording "verified on seven genre holdouts" is incorrect. The macro averaging reads the same way: the eighteen holdouts are eighteen registered split procedures with overlapping test parts, not eighteen independent document sets, so equal weight in the macro goes to procedures, not samples.

#### Data


14. **One-sided text extraction defect.** 97 human documents out of 837 contain verbatim repeated sentences; in the machine part, none of the 1079 documents do. Documents with repetition above 10% are accused by procedure 2 in 49.5% of cases against 11.6% for the rest, and for Lenta the separation is complete: 28 of 28 against 0 of 32. The defect biases the class comparison in one direction and affects more than the classifier: `human_news_lenta_0019` has a mean NLL of 0.886 against a corpus median of 3.09. #### Added 31 July 2026 following the stress test


The rubrics were set by the PI: items 22 and 23 are limitations of measurement and construct validity; item 24 concerns the scope of the robustness check and external validity.

**Limitations of measurement and construct validity**

22. **Hard tercile discretisation amplifies small numerical changes near the boundaries.** `tercile_score` cuts the rank at 1/3 and 2/3, so an input that changed slightly produces a stepwise jump in the index. Confirmed by the computational reproducibility diagnostic: in the 30 July measurement, out of 1853 checks of features M01, M02 and M05 over the panel, 30 values lie within one rank step of a boundary and 9 exactly on it (`status-2026-07-30.md`). This is not an estimate of the effect of the excluded transformation t14.

23. **Format counters are normalised to volume.** R06, R07 and F01 are computed per 1000 words of the `full` profile, P01 as the share of the profile outside connected prose. A change in text length can therefore shift the format score without any change in the absolute number of markup elements. The limitation concerns the operationalisation of the features itself and holds regardless of which transformations entered the final set. The specific numbers on which the mechanism was discovered are moved to the reproducibility appendix with the label invalidated diagnostic run: they were obtained on a run with a transformation recognised as defective and are not a stress-test result.

**Scope of the robustness check and external validity**

24. **The final stress test covers 10 of the 16 registered transformations.** Six intervention-dependent or technically infeasible transformations were not evaluated: five require material the corpus does not contain (the A→H branch, machine rewriting, translation and back-translation, paraphrasing), and the sixth — t14, "removal of typical clichés" — was moved to `not executable` on 31 July because after `prep-v5` preprocessing there is nothing to remove: the registered list of artefacts yields zero hits on the panel. The denominator is 10; there are 600 primary cells. In the paper the result is called a 10-transformation stress test. Amendment: `amendment-stress-r5-t14-not-executable.md`.

#### Added 1 August 2026 following the review of the instability cards


25. **Transformations 10 and 11 are composite, and the effect of volume was not tested by them in isolation.** Both functions split the text into sentences by `\s+` and reassemble it with spaces, so three things change at once: length, verbatim duplication or deletion of sentences, and paragraph markup — the median loss is 83.5% of paragraphs for shortening and 75.6% for expansion. The contributions cannot be separated on these data, and three consequences are recorded directly. First: the effect of volume without duplication has not been tested; the panel contains no transformation that changes length without repeating sentences. Second: the independent effect of paragraph structure is not identified. Third: the direction of the classifier's response is tied to how verbatim repetitions are distributed in the training corpus. A symmetric scan of both parts (`repeat-scan-v2-report.md`) gives an automatic result: **17 human and 0 machine documents exceeded the 0.10 threshold on `prep-v5`**. On `prep-v4` the ratio is the same and sharper — 97 against zero out of 1079; the historical number reproduced exactly; in the machine part the repetition share is zero for all documents, not merely below the threshold. Beyond this the automatic criterion does not go, and the candidates were classified manually into three classes (`repeat-scan-v2-verdicts.md`): 10 documents `confirmed_defect`, 4 `legitimate_source_property`, 3 `unresolved`. The classification was done by a single rater, and this is a limitation of the result. The confirmed defects are block repetitions of fragments in the intermediary dataset `buriy_2014` and a cookie-plugin line; the legitimate repetitions are a doubled bibliography in three SPbU articles, where `References` splits the repetitions exactly in half, and a block of interview questions. A separate mechanism is recorded for `human_news_buriy_2014_0048`: the detachment of the heading by preprocessing made a sentence verbatim equal to the next one, and the repetition appeared where the raw file had none. The material for verification is `repeat-scan-v2-manual.md`, which gives verbatim fragments and structural context for each candidate but no procedure 2 scores, so that the classification would not depend on them. What is established for procedure 2 and for it alone: the decision flip is predominantly associated with an M01 increase of 7.86 σ, and the shift goes towards the "human" class. Under the name M01 the model receives not the mean similarity of adjacent sentences but its standard deviation: `extract_semantic.py` lines 290–292 write the mean to `raw_value` and the dispersion to `normalized_value`, while `clf_run.load_matrix` reads `normalized_value or raw_value`. Duplication places identical phrases side by side with a cosine of 1.0 against a typical 0.5 and raises precisely the dispersion. Why this pulls towards humans is shown by `m01-direction.md`: in the training part the dispersion is systematically higher for human texts — median 0.0907 against 0.0746, Cliff's δ = −0.78 as the median over 18 holdouts — and the M01 coefficient is negative in all eighteen models, with a median rank of 4 out of 22 by absolute value. The mechanism is stated as follows: the model associates a larger dispersion of cosine similarities of adjacent sentences with the human class; verbatim duplication creates extreme adjacent pairs, raises M01 and shifts the decision in that direction. All 109 decision flips go exactly there. The mechanism is corpus- and model-dependent: it describes this classifier on this corpus and does not make verbatim repetition a universal marker of human text. This is a property of this classifier on this corpus, not of duplication as a marker of authorship; for procedure 1 the mechanism requires algebraic decomposition, and for the NLL and the judge it remains unknown. The earlier interpretation via volume is withdrawn and moved to the reproducibility appendix. The analysis: `07-analysis/stress-t10-t11-volume-defect.md`.

26. **The effect of shortening length without a concomitant change in the M01 distribution could not be tested.** A separate transformation was registered for this — "paragraph-preserving truncation without verbatim duplication", shortening by roughly a quarter while preserving paragraph markup and without verbatim duplication (`amendment-sensitivity-truncation.md`). It passed three of the four acceptance criteria: markup preserved in all sixty panel documents, no duplication introduced, median volume ratio 0.7904. The fourth criterion, written down before the computation, was violated: the median Δz for M01 was +0.0079 against the requirement "not above zero", and in ten documents the increase exceeded 0.5 σ with a maximum of +2.855. The transformation was rejected at preflight; procedure 2 was not run on it; there are no sensitivity results. The criterion was not relaxed after seeing the result: an alternative formulation via a systematic shift would have passed on these data for certain, i.e. it would have been fitted to an already known answer. **This is not a null result on the effect of length but the infeasibility of this operationalisation.** A diagnostic obtained after the gate had fired: the shift is symmetric — an increase in thirty documents, a decrease in thirty — whereas for transformation 11 the median Δz is +7.86 with an increase in all sixty; the largest deviations fall on short documents, because M01 is itself a sample standard deviation over twenty to seventy pairs of adjacent sentences. Returning to the question requires a new transformation whose criteria are checked on a separate development panel. The decision and the numbers: `07-analysis/sensitivity-v1-rejection.json`.

### 5. The parse cache key


The Stanza, NER, and embedding caches were addressed by `document_id` alone. During the prep-v5 correction, the text of 68 documents changed while their identifiers stayed the same, and the cache would have returned a stale parse for them.

The preprocessing version is recorded as provenance but does not enter the validity check: a byte-identical input yields a byte-identical parse, and requiring the version to match would have forced recomputation of 1814 documents with no change in the result. The premise was verified — for all 1916 documents, the `prose_sha256` of the version under which the parse was built matched the `prose_sha256` in prep-v4.

New entries do not overwrite old ones: the name contains the input hash, so the prep-v4 parse stays on disk and series v1 remains reproducible.
